AIT Assistant: Exploring Fact-Checking, User Experience, and Usability in AI-Powered Search

Duration: 6 months (Master’s thesis)

My Roles: UX Researcher, UX Designer, Prompt Engineer

Mixed-Methods Research Usability Testing Critical Incident Technique Survey Design Interviewing Thematic Analysis UX Writing Prompt Engineering Design for Trust Design Documentation
Figma

For my HCI master’s thesis, I designed and evaluated AIT Assistant—an experimental, LLM-powered workplace chatbot that helps employees efficiently locate Quality Management documents while exploring novel interface mechanisms for encouraging fact-checking of AI-generated content. I designed the interface in Figma, engineered the prompt for the LLM, and conducted mixed-methods research to examine usability, user experience, and how design and context influence verification behavior. This project deepened my ability to design for responsible AI use and offered insights into how interface features, user expectations, and contextual constraints shape interactions with AI systems in professional settings.

PROBLEM

Users often don’t verify LLM responses

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly relevant in professional environments, where they are used to support tasks like document retrieval, summarization, and question answering [1, 2]. Their appeal lies in their potential to increase productivity by providing information more quickly, easily, and intuitively [3, 4, 5].

Yet alongside these benefits, LLMs carry significant risks. Their outputs are prone to factual errors, inconsistencies, and hallucinations [6, 7]. These issues often go unnoticed due to the models’ plausible-sounding responses, which can mislead users [8]. Furthermore, users may find it difficult to assess the reliability of AI-generated responses, as many current systems lack effective support for fact-checking, and citations—when present—are often missing, unclear, or entirely fabricated [9, 10].

SOLUTION

Design for critical engagement

To address these challenges, I designed AIT Assistant—an experimental, LLM-powered search interface that explored user interface and prompt strategies to support fact-checking in high-stakes environments. The goal was not only to retrieve documents efficiently, but to encourage users to critically engage with AI-generated responses through a combination of user interface features and structured output design.

I created a high-fidelity prototype in Figma and engineered a custom prompt for the LLM with dedicated sections like “Reasoning Behind Answer” and “Information to Fact-Check,” designed to make the model’s logic more transparent and traceable. The interface was designed to prominently display source documents and supporting passages, alongside system limitations communicated through elements such as warning messages.

The final version tested with users was implemented in Svelte by collaborating developers.

RESEARCH

A mixed-methods study with real users

To understand how interface and contextual design influence fact-checking behavior, usability, and user experience, I conducted a mixed-methods study with 16 employees at the Austrian Institute of Technology. Participants interacted with AIT Assistant in realistic scenarios involving the retrieval of Quality Management documents.

The study was composed of four research components:

  • Exploratory usability testing to identify early usability issues and observe initial user interactions with the prototype.
  • Scenario-based usability testing to evaluate how users approached a realistic fact-checking task, focusing on how user interface elements influenced usability, user experience, and fact-checking behavior.
  • Post-task survey to collect quantitative data on usability, user experience, and the perceived helpfulness of user interface elements in supporting fact-checking.
  • Semi-structured interviews to gather deeper insights into users’ motivations to fact-check, perceived usefulness of specific features, situational factors, and overall emotional responses.

I analyzed the data using a combination of the Critical Incident Technique, thematic analysis of interview transcripts, and statistical analysis of survey responses to identify patterns in user behavior, usability pain points, and design insights related to fact-checking.

FINDINGS

Uncovering insights through users’ eyes

AIT Assistant performed well overall in usability (average SUS score: 79.38) and user experience (average UEQ-S score: 1.33) evaluations, demonstrating that designs that promote critical engagement can still offer a high-quality, usable experience.

Key insights included:

  • Trust and verification aren’t mutually exclusive. Even when participants perceived the AI’s answers as trustworthy, many still engaged in fact-checking, especially when stakes felt high or ambiguity was present.
  • Well-placed user interface elements prompted deeper engagement. Elements like warning messages, relevance indicators, and clearly visible source excerpts helped participants recognize when closer scrutiny might be needed and lowered the effort required to follow through.
  • Structured AI responses reduced cognitive load. Breaking answers into sections such as “Reasoning and Assumptions Behind Answer” and “Information to Fact-Check” made it easier for participants to understand the system’s reasoning and assess which parts to examine more critically.

REFLECTION

Learning to listen to data, people, and constraints

Working on AIT Assistant helped me grow in ways I didn’t expect by teaching me how much context, constraints, and human nuance shape whether a design actually works the way you hope it will.

One of the most challenging (yet valuable) parts of the process was making sense of different types of data. Interviews, surveys, and usability tests each revealed something slightly different about how people interacted with the tool, and sometimes those signals didn’t seem to align. I learned not to chase a single “truth,” but to look for the bigger patterns and tensions that helped me understand the full picture. That experience sharpened my judgment and made me a more careful, balanced researcher.

I also came to appreciate the practical side of working with real people in real workplaces. Scheduling and running sessions required a lot of flexibility—people had competing responsibilities, last-minute changes, and limited time. It reminded me how important it is to design research that respects those realities. Piloting the sessions and adjusting the scope made a big difference, and it’s something I’ll carry into future work.

Collaborating with the development team also pushed me to think more carefully about what’s feasible. Some features had to be simplified or left out entirely due to time or technical constraints. That forced me to focus on priorities, like understanding what really supports users, what’s essential for transparency, and what can be adjusted without compromising the experience.

Overall, this project deepened my understanding of what it takes to create responsible, user-centered AI tools. Not just designing features, but shaping the experience around how people actually work, think, and make decisions.
