AI-Powered Search: Designing for Fact-Checking, User Experience, and Usability

Duration: 6 months (Master’s thesis)

My Roles: UX Researcher, UX Designer, Prompt Engineer

Human-AI Interaction Responsible AI LLM Prompt Design Mixed-Methods Research Observation Survey Semi-Structured Interview Thematic Analysis Critical Incident Technique
Figma Excel

For my HCI master’s thesis, I designed and evaluated an LLM-powered workplace search interface that helps employees efficiently find documents while supporting critical engagement with AI-generated content. I designed the interface in Figma, engineered the prompt for the LLM, and conducted a mixed-methods study to assess usability, user experience, and verification behavior. This project deepened my ability to design for responsible AI use and offered insights into how interface features, user expectations, and contextual constraints shape interactions with AI systems in professional settings.

Note: Some project details have been generalized or omitted to respect confidentiality agreements.

PROBLEM

Users often don’t verify LLM responses

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly relevant in professional environments, where they are used to support tasks like document retrieval, summarization, and question answering [1, 2]. Their appeal lies in their potential to increase productivity by providing information more quickly, easily, and intuitively [3, 4, 5].

Yet alongside these benefits, LLMs carry significant risks. Their outputs are prone to factual errors, inconsistencies, and hallucinations [6, 7]. These issues often go unnoticed due to the models’ plausible-sounding responses, which can mislead users [8]. Furthermore, users may find it difficult to assess the reliability of AI-generated responses, as many current systems lack effective support for fact-checking, and citations—when present—are often missing, unclear, or entirely fabricated [9, 10].

SOLUTION

Design for critical engagement

To address these challenges, I designed an experimental LLM-powered search interface while exploring user interface and prompt strategies to support fact-checking in high-stakes environments. The goal was not only to retrieve documents efficiently, but to encourage users to critically engage with AI-generated responses through a combination of user interface features and structured output design.

I created a high-fidelity prototype in Figma and engineered a custom prompt for the LLM designed to support verification. The interface was designed to provide easy access to source documents while maintaining transparency by communicating system limitations.

The final version tested with users was implemented with the help of collaborating developers.

RESEARCH

A mixed-methods study with real users

To understand how interface and contextual design influence fact-checking behavior, usability, and user experience, I conducted a mixed-methods study with 16 participants.

The study was composed of four research components:

  • 1. Exploratory observation to identify early usability issues and observe initial user interactions with the prototype.
  • 2. Scenario-based observation to evaluate how users approached a realistic fact-checking task, focusing on how user interface elements influenced usability, user experience, and fact-checking behavior.
  • 3. A post-task survey to collect quantitative data on usability, user experience, and the perceived helpfulness of user interface elements in supporting fact-checking.
  • 4. Semi-structured interviews to gather deeper insights into users’ motivations to fact-check, perceived usefulness of specific features, situational factors, and overall emotional responses.

I analyzed the data using a combination of the Critical Incident Technique, thematic analysis of interview transcripts, and statistical analysis of survey responses to identify patterns in user behavior, usability pain points, and design insights related to fact-checking.

FINDINGS

What made people trust the AI—yet still fact-check it anyway

The system performed well overall in usability and user experience evaluations, demonstrating that designs that promote critical engagement can still offer a high-quality, usable experience.

Key insights included:

  • Trust and verification aren’t mutually exclusive. Even when participants perceived the AI’s answers as trustworthy, many still engaged in fact-checking, especially when stakes felt high or when ambiguity was present.
  • Clear, static cues prompted engagement. Clear, static components subtly encouraged verification with minimal cognitive effort, while more complex ones led to more mixed reactions.
  • Information structure shaped cognitive load. Participants appreciated structured outputs, but overly long or inconsistently formatted responses caused confusion or disengagement.
  • Perceived authority sometimes backfired. In some cases, explanation features appeared so authoritative and felt so convincing that they discouraged critical engagement, illustrating the risk of automation complacency.

REFLECTION

Listening to data, people, and constraints

One of the most challenging yet valuable parts of engaging in this project was making sense of different types of data. Observation sessions, surveys, and interviews each revealed something slightly different about how people interacted with the tool, and sometimes these data didn’t seem to align. I learned not to chase a single “truth,” but to look for bigger patterns that helped me understand the full picture.

I also came to appreciate the practical side of working with real people in real workplaces. Scheduling and running sessions required a lot of flexibility—people had competing responsibilities, last-minute changes, and limited time. It reminded me how important it is to design research that respects these limitations.

Collaborating with the development team also pushed me to think more carefully about what’s technically feasible. Some features had to be simplified or left out entirely due to time or technical constraints. That forced me to focus on priorities, like understanding what really supports users, what’s essential for transparency, and what can be adjusted without compromising the experience.

Overall, this project deepened my understanding of what it takes to create responsible, user-centered AI tools.
