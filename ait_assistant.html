AIT Assistant: Designing Trustworthy AI for the Workplace

Duration: 6 months (Master’s Thesis)

My Roles: UX researcher, Interaction designer, Prompt engineer

Mixed-Methods Research Usability Testing Critical Incident Technique Survey Design Interviewing Thematic Analysis UX Writing Prompt Engineering Design for Trust Design Documentation
Figma

My master's thesis focused on designing and evaluating AIT Assistant—an LLM-powered workplace chatbot that helps employees locate Quality Management documents while promoting fact-checking. I led the end-to-end research and design process, combining UX methods with LLM prompt engineering to create an interface that balanced efficiency, transparency, and trust.

PROBLEM

Users often trust LLMs—but don’t verify

Despite the risks of hallucinations, users rarely verify information provided by LLMs. Prior studies (e.g. Budiu et al., 2023) showed minimal fact-checking in commercial systems like ChatGPT. I aimed to investigate how interface and contextual factors influence trust, usability, and verification behavior in an LLM-powered workplace tool.

SOLUTION

Designing a verification-friendly chatbot

I designed a prototype that foregrounded document transparency and encouraged verification using sections like "Passages Used to Generate Answer," "Reasoning and Assumptions," and a persistent "Warning Message." These were complemented by prompt-engineered AI responses structured for clarity and traceability.

RESEARCH

Mixed-methods study with real users

I conducted a three-phase study with 16 employees at the Austrian Institute of Technology:

  • Exploratory usability testing
  • Scenario-based fact-checking tasks
  • Post-task surveys and in-depth interviews
I applied the Critical Incident Technique, ISO 9241-11 usability metrics, and thematic coding to analyze behavioral patterns, interface pain points, and design implications.

FINDINGS

Trust and verification can coexist

Participants engaged in verification despite trusting the AI, especially when interface cues like document excerpts and relevance bars were salient. However, overly long AI responses and poor visual hierarchy reduced usability and discouraged engagement. Key findings:

  • Trust was driven by ability, integrity, and transparency.
  • Verification was triggered by task importance, unclear answers, or design cues.
  • Warnings and side-by-side layouts improved user awareness and behavior.

RESULT

Design lessons for LLM-integrated tools

The final prototype received strong usability scores (SUS = 79.38) and pragmatic UEQ-S scores. My work contributed design implications for future AI interfaces:

  • Communicating limitations can build trust—when well integrated
  • Fact-checking should be easy, intuitive, and encouraged by design
  • Structured AI responses help reduce cognitive overload

REFLECTION

Design is not just features—it’s context

This project taught me that even well-intentioned features can fail if users don’t understand or notice them. I learned to consider placement, visual prominence, and clarity as central to usable transparency. Collaborating closely with developers also helped me iterate design decisions based on LLM limitations and response latency, deepening my cross-functional skills.
